# The double-slit experiment

**Topic:** Quantum Physics

**Module:** Wave-particle duality

Fire electrons at a barrier one at a time and something impossible happens. Each
one lands as a single dot, clearly a particle, yet after thousands of arrivals
those dots assemble into the bright-and-dark stripes of a wave. This is where
wave–particle duality stops being a slogan and becomes something you can
measure.

## What actually happens

The apparatus is deliberately simple: a source, a barrier with two narrow slits,
and a detector screen. You send particles through and record where each one
lands.

- Fire the electrons **one at a time**, so no two are ever inside the apparatus
  together.
- Each electron is detected as a single, localised dot. It never splits or
  smears.
- Yet the pattern that builds up over thousands of electrons is an
  **interference pattern** of alternating bright and dark fringes.
- Cover one slit and the fringes vanish. You get a single broad smear instead.

Each electron behaves as though it passed through **both** slits and interfered
with itself. That only makes sense if we describe it as a spread-out wave of
probability rather than a tiny ball following one path.

> **Key idea:** A quantum object is neither a wave nor a particle. It travels
> like a spread-out wave of possibilities and is detected like a particle, and
> which face you see is decided by whether you measure the path it took.

## Particles have a wavelength

In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that if light waves can behave like particles,
then particles should behave like waves. He assigned every object a wavelength
set by its momentum:

    λ = h / p

Here **h** is Planck's constant and **p** is momentum. For a cricket ball the
wavelength is unimaginably small, which is why you never see one diffract. For
an electron it is about the size of an atom, large enough that the wave
behaviour dominates, and the interference pattern appears.
