# The Italian Renaissance

**Topic:** Art History

**Module:** The Italian Renaissance

Between roughly 1400 and 1600, artists in the city-states of Italy reinvented
how a picture could work. A painted panel stopped being a flat arrangement of
holy symbols and became a window onto a believable, measured world — one with
depth, weight, and light. This is the moment European art learned to look real.

## What actually changed

The shift was technical as much as it was cultural. A handful of new tools let
painters build convincing space and bodies on a flat surface.

- **Linear perspective** gave painters a geometric system for depth: parallel
  lines converge on a single vanishing point, so a floor recedes and figures sit
  correctly in a room.
- **Chiaroscuro** — the modelling of form through gradations of light and shade
  — made figures look round and solid rather than outlined and flat.
- **Oil paint**, adopted from the north, dried slowly enough to blend colours
  and build luminous, layered glazes.
- **Anatomy and antiquity**: close study of the human body and of classical
  sculpture gave figures naturalistic proportion and movement.

Together these turned the picture plane into a stage. A painting could now stage
a moment — a gesture, a glance — as if you were present for it.

> **Key idea:** The Renaissance was not simply "better drawing." It was a new
> claim that a painting could reconstruct the visible world by rule and
> observation, and that the artist was a thinker whose work deserved a name.

## Patronage and the rise of the artist

Great works needed money, and money came from the Church, wealthy families, and
civic guilds. In Florence the Medici bankrolled artists and shaped taste; in
Rome the papacy commissioned the Sistine Chapel and the new St Peter's.

This patronage changed the artist's standing. Figures like Leonardo,
Michelangelo, and Raphael were no longer anonymous craftsmen but celebrated
individuals, courted by rulers and remembered by name — the beginning of the
idea of the artist as a creative genius.
